# Chapter 7: Subsequent Drafts — From the Page into the Reader's Brain

## Core Idea
Run the first draft through two editing passes: (1) the Academizer — convert casual language to graduate-level prose; (2) sit in the reader's brain — correct obvious gaffes without overanalyzing or losing authentic voice.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Academizer**: An editing pass that converts first-draft casual language into the register a scholar would use writing to a colleague. Requires revisiting textbooks, looking up citations, and using terminology with precision.
  - Example: "I had a part-time work-study job in the biology department" → "I assisted Dr. R. Simmons on a study of possible medicinal derivatives of the venom of the southern copperhead, Agkistrodon contortrix."
  - The Academizer dial is set to 11: systematically rewrite every casual construction.
- **Sitting in the Reader's Brain**: Read each section and ask "how will this reader respond?" — correct only the obvious gaffes (business school reader doesn't want to hear about moving away from family; wants to hear about contract terms saved). Do not overanalyze or you'll destroy authenticity.
- **Essay Hall of Shame** (direct quotes from admissions readers):
  - "Errors and sloppiness"
  - "Anything that starts with 'I've always wanted to be a ___'"
  - "They don't really answer the question"
  - "When they're just saying what they think we want to hear"
  - "Recycling material from other schools"
  - "Claims of altruism with nothing in the application to back them up"
  - "Too long — it shows no discipline"
  - "Wrong school name"
  - "All activities are brand new — red flag"
  - "Students too afraid to take risks that don't tell us anything — throws us back on the numbers"
- **Weaknesses-as-Strengths Formula**: When asked about weaknesses, describe strengths turned inside-out: "I study too much and forget to have fun," "I sometimes spend too much time in the library." Admissions readers are tired of this approach but still prefer it over genuine weaknesses.

## Key Concepts
- **Graduate-Level Language Register**: MFA essays can be conversational but should discuss artistic concepts with sophistication. Law essays benefit from legal citations and terminology. Medical essays improve with medical terms used doctor-to-doctor. Science essays are often highly impersonal.
- **Judgment as the Subtext**: Admissions committees are evaluating your judgment through what you reveal. Disclosing you are in litigation with your advisor, or claiming your status as a Christian is a medical qualification, are signals of poor judgment.
- **Arrogance-to-Humility Language Conversion**: "I know I would be an outstanding addition to your student body" → "I would love to become a tireless and valuable contributor." "Buildings will never make sense until architects learn to…" → "As an architect, my passionate interest will be to improve…"
- **Character Weakness Prohibition**: Sloth, dishonesty, egocentricity — if any of these are visible, revise. Never write that you drink too much, lack motivation, or are frequently depressed.
- **Ongoing Mental Anguish Ban**: Writing about personal problems is potentially powerful; writing about *ongoing* mental anguish is not. Past triumph over adversity is acceptable; current suffering is not.
- **Sexist Language Audit**: Required editing pass — taught in high school, no excuse for getting it wrong.
- **Spicing Up**: After the reader's-brain pass, read again for lines you can add life to with detail and accomplishment ("I worked as a teacher's aide" → specific story of what happened).

## Mental Models
- "If sitting in the reader's brain causes you to throw out most of your first draft, you are being too critical" — only correct obvious gaffes
- Late in admissions season, some committees don't read past the first mistake. Early submission gives you the fresh, alert, compassionate reader; late submission gives you the exhausted one.
- One well-crafted essay can push a borderline candidate into the admit pile; one Hall of Shame moment can knock a strong candidate out

## Anti-patterns
- **Overanalyzing during the reader's-brain pass**: Leads to sanitized, generic essays that "don't tell us anything" — exactly what admissions readers complain about
- **Staying too casual after first draft**: The first-draft voice is a starting point, not the final register
- **Describing truly revealing weaknesses**: "I drink too much" is begging for rejection
- **Criticizing past professors, programs, or your current institution**: Signals poor judgment regardless of the facts
- **Starting over after the reader's-brain edit**: You'll lose authenticity; edit, don't rewrite

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the Academizer pass first: systematically convert casual language to graduate-level register.
2. Sit in the reader's brain second: correct obvious gaffes only, do not over-sanitize.
3. Wrong school name, generic openers, recycled essays, claims not backed by the rest of the application — all Hall of Shame.
4. Your judgment is being evaluated through what you choose to reveal.
5. Arrogance in language signals inflexibility; convert "must" to "would greatly benefit," "I know" to "I would love."
6. Show only the second draft or later to other humans — never the first draft.
