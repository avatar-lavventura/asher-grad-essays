# Chapter 8: Samples, Samples, Samples

## Core Idea
Fifty+ annotated real essays across all program types. Browse the entire collection before focusing on your field. Never model closely — admissions readers have this book.

## Key Concepts
- **Anti-Modeling Warning**: "Do not even think about modeling your essay closely on one of the examples in this book; essays are provided so you may learn how to write the best statement for your own unique background. Admissions people have also seen this book, and if you copy material, they will notice right away."
- **Browse Before Filtering**: Read essays across fields, not just yours. The storytelling techniques transfer across disciplines.
- **Annotation Format**: Each essay is accompanied by parenthetical inline commentary from Asher that highlights what worked and why.

## Patterns Across All Strong Essays
From the annotated samples, the following techniques appear repeatedly in successful essays:

1. **Personal hook + academic exposition combination**: The personal information creates reader interest; the academic content proves that interest is warranted. Neither alone is sufficient.
2. **Matter-of-fact adversity tone**: Strong essays mention challenges without asking for special consideration — they explain decision-making processes and turning points, not hardship.
3. **Named mentors and advisors**: "Always name your advisors and mentors." Their names add credibility and specificity.
4. **Specific research described with precision**: Name the project, the methodology used, the output. "Biologically Unsound: Women, Murder, and the Insanity Plea in the Progressive Era" beats "I wrote a research paper."
5. **Named professors at target program**: The best field-specific essays identify 2–3 faculty at the target school and explain why their specific work aligns with the applicant's interests.
6. **Publication readiness**: If a paper is good enough, submit for publication before applying. Pending publications count. "A paper currently pending publication in [journal]" is a credential.
7. **Epiphany structure**: The moment the decision crystallized — specific place, specific sensory detail, specific realization.
8. **Science essays can be personal OR highly impersonal**: Neither is "right." The key is alignment with background, topic, and institution.

## Essay Style Spectrum
- **Personal narrative dominant**: Best for humanities, fine arts, some social sciences. Rich personal story + academic thread.
- **Research-focused impersonal**: Best for hard sciences, engineering. Hypothesis, methodology, specific lab experience, specific program faculty.
- **Professional intent + credential**: Best for law, business, medicine. Career goal stated directly, evidence of relevant experience, specific program fit.
- **Hybrid**: Personal epiphany → research exposition → specific program alignment. Works across most fields.

## Anti-patterns Illustrated in the Samples
- Essays that read as résumé recitation (activities listed without what was learned)
- Essays that praise the program ("Cornell has an exciting interdisciplinary program") without specificity about which professors, which research, which aspect
- Opening lines that begin "I always wanted to be a ___"
- Essays that explain what the applicant's field is — admissions readers know their own discipline

## Key Takeaways
1. Browse all 50 essays regardless of your field before reading within your field.
2. The personal + academic combination is the most common structure of the strongest essays.
3. Name mentors, name professors at the target school, name research projects with working titles.
4. Adversity essays work when the tone is matter-of-fact, not when they ask for special consideration.
5. Science essays can be personal or impersonal — match the style to the field's norms.
6. Never copy; the annotated essays teach technique, not content.
