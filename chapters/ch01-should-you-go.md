# Chapter 1: Should You Go to Grad School, and How Are You Going to Pay for This?

## Core Idea
Before applying, diagnose whether your motivations are solid and understand the counterintuitive rules of graduate financing — particularly that expensive schools are often cheaper, and PhD funding often beats master's funding.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Good Reasons vs. Warning Signs**: Structured decision filter before committing
  - Good reasons: passionate interest in narrow topic, vocational credential requirement, earning potential, career advancement, quality of life, research love, natural aptitude for school
  - Warning signs (3): (1) going to please someone else, (2) clueless about a subject, (3) secretly avoiding the job market
- **The Lifetime Earnings Argument**: Bureau of Labor Statistics data: PhD adds ~$1M, professional degree adds ~$1.4M, master's adds $400K over bachelor's — "the second-best investment after your bachelor's"
- **Funding Counterintuitions**: Five rules that surprise most applicants:
  1. You cannot know cost until admitted
  2. Expensive schools are often cheaper (deeper aid resources)
  3. PhD is often cheaper than master's (PhD candidates are better funded)
  4. Full time is often cheaper than part time (compounding loan payoff vs. stagnant salary)
  5. Apply for aid even if wealthy (merit fellowships build credentials, TAs are required for academic careers)
- **Bailout Master's Trap**: Do not pursue a PhD when you want a master's just because PhD funding is better — "dropping out of a PhD program" carries a permanent stigma

## Key Concepts
- **Teaching Assistantship (TA)**: Tuition waiver + stipend in exchange for teaching undergraduates; highly prestigious for future professors; deadlines are often months before the program deadline
- **Research Assistantship (RA)**: Assigned to a specific professor/lab; easier to win than TA (impress one person, not a department); GPA in your specialty matters more than overall GPA
- **Fellowship**: Large award, no strings, reserved for top candidates or underrepresented groups; internal (from school) and external (third-party); science applicants expected to apply for 3–4 external fellowships
- **Assistantship Visit Protocol**: If invited to tour, this is not optional — assistantships are often assigned informally on these visits; ask them to pay for your ticket if needed
- **Financial Aid vs. Funding**: "Financial aid" at grad level usually means loans; ask about "funding and support" to access scholarships, fellowships, and assistantships

## Mental Models
- "Getting What You Came For" by Robert Peters: go-to resource for academic funding, advisor selection, and departmental politics
- The loan-appeal maneuver: if you have competing offers, use them to negotiate; some deans who "never" match offers actually do when the difference is small (~$5K)

## Anti-patterns
- **Pursuing PhD for funding alone**: Leads to dropping out mid-program; industry views "bailout master's" as a PhD dropout
- **Employer-sponsored education as free money**: Employers pay below market salary to subsidize it; run the numbers before assuming you're ahead
- **Not applying early for assistantships**: They have separate, earlier deadlines and sometimes require separate essays and different letters of recommendation

## Key Takeaways
1. The single best reason to go is passionate interest in a subject — everything else is secondary.
2. You cannot know cost until admitted; always apply for aid regardless of means.
3. Expensive schools often cost less due to deeper endowments.
4. PhD candidates are typically better funded than master's candidates in the same program.
5. Apply for 3–4 external fellowships in science; this is an unwritten expectation.
6. If visiting for an assistantship tour, treat it as mandatory.
