# Chapter 2: Choosing a School or Program

## Core Idea
Build a list of 20+ programs nationally, evaluate on five dimensions (not just rankings), and make direct contact with faculty before applying. Programs matter more than institutions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 20-School Rule**: Start with at least 20 possibilities nationally; you will not apply to all of them, but you need this breadth to find the right set of 6–10 to actually apply to
  - Why national: "reach" matters to programs (geographic diversity is an advantage); and programs may pay you to relocate
- **Five-Dimension School Evaluation**: For each program, assess: (1) academics (intellectual fit, faculty research), (2) people (faculty, current students, administration attitude), (3) locale, (4) cost/funding, (5) admissibility
- **Department vs. Institution Rule**: At the grad level, only programs have reputations. An outstanding program can be inside an otherwise undistinguished institution, and a paralyzed department can exist inside an elite university. Always evaluate the department, not the school.
- **How to Approach a Graduate School** (Asher's funnel):
  1. University home page → department home page → specific labs/subdisciplines → individual faculty home pages → current advisees (enrolled grad students)
  2. Identify 1–4 faculty who publish, win grants, and align with your interests
  3. Write to faculty by email asking specific, well-informed questions
- **Law of Descending Prestige**: In academic hiring, you will typically teach at an institution with less prestige than the one that granted your degree. If you want a faculty career, the prestige of your program matters.
- **Rankings Skepticism Framework**: Reputations lag 3–5 years behind reality. Gossip from faculty and current grad students is often more current. Peer-reviewed article counts per faculty, and who cites whom, is a more rigorous proxy than magazine rankings.

## Key Concepts
- **Accreditation**: CHEA (Council for Higher Education Accreditation) maintains a searchable database. If an institution is not there, do not attend. Diploma mills look legitimate.
- **Reach**: A program's stated pride in drawing students from many states. Applying from an underrepresented region is a genuine advantage.
- **Rolling Admissions**: Applications reviewed as they arrive; slots fill as the year progresses. Earlier is always better for rolling-admissions programs.
- **Strong Graduate School vs. Strong Department Model**: Harvard = strong departments, department decides. Duke = strong graduate school, grad school can override departments. Affects where to direct your energy.
- **Front-Loading Aid Warning**: Some programs give heavy first-year packages to win competition, then reduce in later years. Ask: "How reliable is your financial aid year to year?"

## Mental Models
- Use currently enrolled grad students as information sources: they know who's hot, who's overrated, which programs lost a superstar faculty recently
- Read academic journals in your field and track where the cutting-edge authors teach — that's your "A list"
- Build your own ranking by looking at per-capita peer-reviewed articles and cross-citation patterns between schools

## Anti-patterns
- **Choosing "the best program I can get into"**: There is no single hierarchy; fit matters more than rank
- **Looking only within 4-hour drive**: Most students do this; it dramatically limits both options and funding
- **Fixating on rankings**: They reflect 3–5 year-old conditions; project long-term trends instead
- **Confusing institutional reputation with program reputation**: A Number-One school's department may be stagnant; a Number-Thirty school's department may be thriving

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with 20+ programs nationally; local-only search puts you at competitive disadvantage.
2. Evaluate programs on five dimensions, not just reputation or rankings.
3. Go to faculty home pages and read their recent work before contacting them.
4. Gossip from current grad students is often more accurate than published rankings.
5. Accreditation is non-negotiable; always verify via CHEA before committing.
6. If you want a faculty career, the Law of Descending Prestige means prestige of your program does matter.
