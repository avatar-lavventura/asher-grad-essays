# Chapter 6: Your First Draft — From Your Heart onto the Page

## Core Idea
Answer the question honestly and directly, in your own voice, without editing. Write when you write; edit when you edit. The first draft must come from the heart — revisions come in Chapter 7.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **RTGDQ**: "Read The Gosh Darn Question." The three common essay prompts are all variants of: Where are you coming from? Why are you coming here? Where are you going? Answer the question as asked, in the order asked.
  - When to use: Before writing a single word, and again midway through each draft
  - How: Literally open the application to the exact wording and keep it visible while you write
- **Write-First, Edit-Second Phase Separation**: First draft = content from heart, no judgment. Second draft and beyond = edit for reader. Mixing these phases corrupts both.
  - Practical: voice-record, or write as a letter to a friend — anything to bypass the internal editor
- **Interesting Opening Inventory** (choose what fits your background):
  1. Epiphany: the moment you decided on this path, or first saw what the field really entailed
  2. Self-definition in one sentence: "I am the sixth of seven children from a large blue-collar Irish-Catholic family in the Midwest"
  3. Bold position: open with a claim that promises more to follow
  4. Vivid specific detail: the urinating patient, the kittiwake flush call — detail that proves the writer is serious even while unusual
- **School-Specific Demonstration Technique**: Prove you researched this program specifically by naming professors and citing their work. Find where your philosophy and theirs meet in "happy confluence."
- **Reductionism (Use Sparingly)**: Numbered lists are efficient and signal clear thinking, but a whole essay of them is exhausting. One or two numbered passages per essay is appropriate.

## Key Concepts
- **Essay Question Universality**: Most schools ask essentially the same questions with different wording. Write for your first-choice school first, then adapt for others.
- **Detail over Listing**: One rich description of one incident is worth more than a listing of activities. "I worked as a teacher's aide" vs. a specific story of what happened in that classroom.
- **Vague Adjective Prohibition**: Avoid meaningful, challenging, beautiful, wonderful, invaluable, rewarding. Replace each with a specific "I felt / I realized / I saw that" statement.
- **Footnotes**: Avoid them.
- **Narrative Exposition**: Avoid labyrinthine academic-style prose. Direct, confident voice.
- **Not Redundant with Other Application Parts**: Do not re-list what's already in your resume; instead, give new perspective on those experiences and tie to the rest of your application with "see letter of recommendation / see resume / see CV."
- **Explaining Unknown Honors**: If your honor or program is not well-known, explain its rigor with precision ("a rigorous program that has historically produced students who succeed in graduate environments") — self-referential claims of eliteness fall flat, but specific descriptions of what it required do not.

## Mental Models
- "Assume you will be admitted" — write with confidence, not hedging
- The best opening line gives the reader a gift; it signals that what follows is worth their time
- Three essay types: personal (MFA, medicine can be conversational), technical/scientific (impersonal, methodology-focused), and hybrid (personal hook + academic exposition)

## Anti-patterns
- **Generic opener**: "I always wanted to be a ___" — AMCAS readers call this the sentence they have come to hate most
- **Not answering the question**: RTGDQ exists because students routinely fail to do this
- **Second-guessing the reader during the first draft**: Save that for Ch 7. Let the first draft be authentic.
- **Showing the first draft to anyone**: Wait for a revised second draft before getting feedback
- **Sending the same essay to every school**: Must be adapted; each program must see evidence of specific research

## Key Takeaways
1. RTGDQ: read the exact question before and during drafting.
2. Write the first draft entirely from your own voice and heart — no editing, no reader-guessing.
3. The epiphany structure is the strongest type of opening for most programs.
4. Specific, vivid detail beats listing of accomplishments every time.
5. Demonstrate school-specific research: name professors, cite their work, show where your interests meet theirs.
6. Avoid all vague adjectives (meaningful, challenging, rewarding) — replace with specific "I" statements.
